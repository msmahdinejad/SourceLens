# Open Questions in Retail EV Charging

### **How EV Fast Charging at Stores Turns an Everyday Shopping Stop into Useful Road-Trip Time**
*What Drivers, Retailers, and Charging Planners Need to Know*

---

#### **Key Insights for Drivers, Retailers, and Planners**

##### **1. For Drivers: Fast Charging at Stores Saves Time and Reduces Range Anxiety**
- **Preference for DC fast charging (DCFC):** Drivers strongly prefer **DC fast charging (DCFC)** over AC charging for public stations, especially during long-distance trips, because it significantly shortens charging time [S1]. Terms like *"DC charging," "short time,"* and *"public charging pile"* are closely associated with consumer discussions about charging, while *"too slow"* remains a top complaint [S1].
- **Convenience of retail charging:** Stores like Walmart and Sam’s Club, which offer **fast chargers on-site**, allow drivers to combine charging with shopping, eating, or running errands—turning idle time into productive time [S3][S10]. This aligns with driver behavior: **90% of Americans live within 10 miles of a Walmart**, making retail charging a practical solution for both local and road-trip charging [S10].
- **Cost concerns:** While DCFC is preferred for speed, **AC charging remains popular for home use** due to its lower cost [S1]. Retailers offering **"Every Day Low Price" charging** (e.g., Walmart) can attract cost-conscious EV owners [S3][S10].

**Driver Takeaway:**
- Use **DC fast chargers at retail locations** for road trips to minimize downtime.
- Take advantage of **bundled activities** (shopping, dining) while charging to make the most of your time.
- If cost is a priority, **AC charging at home** or overnight is still the most economical option [S1].

---

##### **2. For Retailers: Fast Charging Attracts Customers and Boosts Revenue**
- **Increased foot traffic and dwell time:** Retailers like **Ricker’s (Indianapolis)** and **Walmart** report **steady growth in customer visits** after installing fast chargers [S5][S12]. Drivers who charge at stores are more likely to **spend money on-site**, whether on groceries, meals, or other retail products [S3][S10].
- **Competitive advantage:** Retailers that offer **fast, reliable, and affordable charging** can differentiate themselves in a growing EV market. For example, **Nissan’s "No Charge to Charge" program** helped Ricker’s become a market leader in EV infrastructure, attracting new customers [S5][S12].
- **Low-cost entry points:** Programs like **Nissan’s No Charge to Charge** or **government incentives** can reduce the financial burden of installing chargers, making it easier for retailers to enter the EV market [S5][S12].

**Retailer Takeaway:**
- Install **DC fast chargers** to attract EV drivers and increase on-site spending.
- Partner with **automakers or charging networks** (e.g., Tesla, Nissan) to reduce upfront costs [S5][S12].
- Offer **bundled promotions** (e.g., discounts for shoppers who charge) to maximize customer engagement [S3][S10].

---

##### **3. For Charging Planners: Designing Infrastructure for Speed, Accessibility, and Grid Reliability**
- **Prioritize fast charging for road trips:** Planners should focus on **high-power DCFC (150–350 kW)** for retail and highway locations, as drivers prefer the **fastest available charging** when away from home [S4][S11]. However, **AC charging (L2) remains critical for residential and workplace settings** [S4].
- **Grid-friendly charging:** As EV adoption grows, **smart charging and battery storage** can help manage grid demand, especially in **urban, rural, and capacity-constrained areas** [S2][S6]. For example, **battery energy storage systems** can support fast-charging stations during peak demand [S6].
- **Standardization and interoperability:** Planners should adopt **international standards** (e.g., **IEC 61851, ISO 15118**) to ensure compatibility across vehicles and charging networks [S9]. This prevents **stranded assets** and improves reliability [S9][S16].
- **Accessibility and site selection:** Charging stations should be **easily accessible, well-lit, and safe**, with clear signage and **ADA-compliant design** [S6][S13]. Retail locations are ideal because they offer **amenities (restrooms, food, shopping)** that enhance the charging experience [S3][S10].

**Planner Takeaway:**
- Deploy **DC fast chargers (150–350 kW)** at retail and highway locations to meet road-trip demand [S4][S11].
- Use **battery storage and smart charging** to mitigate grid strain [S2][S6].
- Follow **international standards** (e.g., IEC, ISO) to ensure interoperability and avoid stranded assets [S9][S16].
- Prioritize **accessible, safe, and amenity-rich locations** (e.g., retail stores) to improve driver satisfaction [S3][S6][S10].

### **Evidence-Backed Research Gaps**
*(Ranked by evidence strength and importance)*

#### **Gap 1: Optimal Charging Speed for Retail Locations**
**What’s missing?**
- While drivers prefer **the fastest available charging (e.g., 350 kW DCFC)** for road trips, there is **no consensus on the "sweet spot" for retail charging** [S1][S4][S11]. Retailers need to balance **speed, cost, and grid impact**—but current data does not specify whether **150 kW, 250 kW, or 350 kW** is the most cost-effective for stores.
- **Why it matters:** Over-investing in ultra-fast chargers could strain the grid and increase costs, while under-investing may frustrate drivers and reduce foot traffic [S2][S6].
- **Testable next questions:**
  - *For retailers:* What is the **minimum charging speed** that satisfies 80% of EV drivers at retail locations?
  - *For planners:* How does **charging speed (150 kW vs. 350 kW)** affect **grid stability and infrastructure costs** in urban vs. rural areas?
- **Method ideas:**
  - **Driver surveys:** Ask EV owners about their **minimum acceptable charging speed** for retail stops (e.g., "Would you wait 10 minutes for 80% charge at a grocery store?").
  - **Pilot studies:** Test **150 kW vs. 350 kW chargers** at the same retail location and measure **driver satisfaction, dwell time, and grid impact**.

---

#### **Gap 2: Driver Behavior During Retail Charging**
**What’s missing?**
- While it’s clear that **drivers prefer fast charging at retail locations**, there is **limited data on how they spend time while charging** [S1][S3][S10]. Do they **shop, eat, or wait in their cars**? How does **dwell time** correlate with **charging speed**?
- **Why it matters:** Retailers need to know **how to design stores and promotions** to maximize revenue from EV drivers. For example, if drivers **only shop for 10 minutes** while charging, retailers should prioritize **grab-and-go items** over sit-down dining.
- **Testable next questions:**
  - *For retailers:* What **percentage of EV drivers** make a purchase while charging, and what **types of products** do they buy?
  - *For planners:* How does **charging speed** affect **dwell time and spending behavior** (e.g., do faster chargers reduce shopping time)?
- **Method ideas:**
  - **In-store observations:** Track **shopping behavior** of EV drivers at retail charging stations (e.g., time spent, items purchased).
  - **Driver diaries:** Ask EV owners to **log their activities** while charging at retail locations (e.g., "I bought a coffee and waited in the car").

---

#### **Gap 3: Cost-Effectiveness of Retail Charging Business Models**
**What’s missing?**
- Retailers like **Walmart and Ricker’s** have successfully deployed fast chargers, but there is **no standardized financial model** for retail charging [S3][S5][S10][S13]. Key questions remain:
  - What is the **break-even point** for retail charging stations (e.g., number of daily users, electricity costs)?
  - How do **pricing models** (e.g., free charging for shoppers, pay-per-use, subscription) affect **driver adoption and retailer revenue**?
- **Why it matters:** Without clear financial models, retailers may **over- or under-invest** in charging infrastructure, leading to **underutilized stations or missed revenue opportunities**.
- **Testable next questions:**
  - *For retailers:* What is the **minimum number of daily charging sessions** needed to justify the cost of a **150 kW DCFC station**?
  - *For planners:* How do **different pricing models** (e.g., free for shoppers, pay-per-use) affect **driver behavior and retailer profits**?
- **Method ideas:**
  - **Cost-benefit analysis:** Compare **installation and operating costs** of retail charging stations against **revenue from increased foot traffic and sales**.
  - **Pilot programs:** Test **different pricing models** (e.g., free charging for shoppers vs. pay-per-use) at the same retail location and measure **driver adoption and retailer revenue**.

---

#### **Gap 4: Grid Impact of Retail Fast Charging**
**What’s missing?**
- While **smart charging and battery storage** can mitigate grid strain, there is **limited real-world data** on how **retail fast charging** affects **local distribution grids** [S2][S6]. Key questions:
  - How does **simultaneous fast charging** (e.g., 5+ cars charging at 150 kW) impact **grid reliability** in urban vs. rural areas?
  - What **grid upgrades** are needed to support **retail fast charging at scale**?
- **Why it matters:** Without proper planning, **retail fast charging could overload local grids**, leading to **blackouts or costly upgrades** [S2][S6].
- **Testable next questions:**
  - *For planners:* What is the **maximum number of fast chargers** that can be deployed at a single retail location **without grid upgrades**?
  - *For utilities:* How does **retail fast charging** compare to **residential or workplace charging** in terms of **grid impact**?
- **Method ideas:**
  - **Grid simulations:** Model the impact of **retail fast charging** on local distribution grids under **different adoption scenarios** (e.g., 10% vs. 50% EV penetration).
  - **Pilot studies:** Deploy **fast chargers at retail locations** and monitor **grid performance** in real time.

---

#### **Gap 5: Consumer Acceptance of "As Fast as Necessary" Charging**
**What’s missing?**
- Current industry consensus is that drivers prefer **"as fast as possible" charging**, but there is **no research on whether they would accept slower charging if incentivized** [S4][S11]. For example:
  - Would drivers **wait 20 minutes instead of 10** if offered **discounts on charging or retail purchases**?
  - How does **charging speed** affect **driver satisfaction and repeat usage**?
- **Why it matters:** **"As fast as necessary" charging** could **reduce infrastructure costs and grid strain**, but it may **frustrate drivers** if not implemented carefully [S4][S11].
- **Testable next questions:**
  - *For retailers:* Would drivers accept **slower charging** if offered **discounts on retail purchases**?
  - *For planners:* How does **charging speed** affect **driver satisfaction and likelihood of repeat usage**?
- **Method ideas:**
  - **Driver surveys:** Ask EV owners about their **willingness to accept slower charging** in exchange for **discounts or other incentives**.
  - **Pilot programs:** Test **"as fast as necessary" pricing models** at retail locations and measure **driver satisfaction and repeat usage**.
